## Polish Note #1 — The Crystal Reveal (parking garage)

**The question:** Crystal currently delivers the emotional thesis for both men
(Tommy, the flask, "killing yourselves in stereo"). Alternative: Ricky confesses
the Tommy backstory himself, badly, mid-argument — costs Crystal's best scene,
gains Ricky depth.

**RULING: Protect Crystal's scene. Do not move the confession to Ricky.**

Reasoning:
- The film's thesis is that Dale and Ricky *cannot say the true thing to each
  other* — thirty years of floorboards. If Ricky can articulate the Tommy wound in
  Act 2, the bridge scene in Act 3 ("I threw the party, Dale") loses its status as
  the first time he says it TO DALE. The garage scene works precisely because the
  truth has to be delivered by the one character who charges by the hour to hear it.
- Crystal saying it also pays off her character logic: she prices everything,
  including this — "So is watching you two idiots kill yourselves in stereo, but
  here we all are" is her paying retail for once. Cut the scene and her Act 3
  choice of Ricky has no foundation.
- **The surgical add that gets us Ricky's depth anyway:** one 4-line beat in the
  bickering montage — Ricky starts a genie hypothetical about "bringing one person
  back," aborts it himself mid-sentence, changes the subject to bidets. Dale clocks
  it, says nothing. Plant, don't pay. The garage remains the payoff.

## Polish Note #2 — Larry's Edge

**The question:** Larry is 90% comic menace / 10% grief. Push darker (one scene
where the politeness fully drops) or keep purely funny?

**RULING: Split the difference — one CRACK, never a drop. Fear level: 7/10, for
exactly eight seconds of screen time.**

Reasoning:
- If the politeness fully drops, Larry becomes an ordinary crime boss and the
  film loses its best joke AND its best threat simultaneously. The menace is the
  manners. What we can afford — and need, so Act 3's forgiveness carries weight —
  is one moment where we see what the manners are *holding back*.
- **Placement:** the first Flapjack Cabin scene already has the seam — "My mother
  came back... heavier." Extend the crack by one beat: Larry's hand flattens on
  the table, the diner ambience drops out of the mix entirely (first time in the
  film Larry exists without cutlery sounds), two seconds of a face nobody should
  see across a breakfast table — then the ambience fades back in and he offers
  boysenberry. No dialogue added. Pure performance, sound, and the push-in
  freezing mid-move.
- This calibrates "more than disappointed, Dale" from a punchline into a promise,
  raises Act 2's stakes without a new scene, and makes the Act 3 vise-opening
  (the push-in stopping halfway) land as real grace.

## Polish Note #3 — Vice Calibration (Ricky's coke)

**The question:** Raunchier (hard-R, *Wolf of Wall Street* energy) or more
restrained in Act 1?

**RULING: Keep current calibration. Resist the hard-R pull.**

Reasoning:
- The film's contract with the audience is that vice is funny *because it is small,
  habitual, and Midwestern* — a key bump in a Walmart parking lot at 11 PM, called
  "a vitamin." Wolf of Wall Street energy glamorizes; this film's comedy depends on
  the vice never once looking like a good time, only like a routine. The casino
  bender is the ceiling ("SEVEN HOURS LATER," the cowboy hat, "DOM-I-NIC") and it
  already plays as mania, not fun — note we arrive AFTER the party and find Ricky
  crying next to a ginger ale. That structure (skip the high, land on the crash)
  IS the calibration. Protect it.
- One restraint-side trim to consider on the day: Act 1 needs only two on-screen
  bumps (parking lot, pre-Stor-Mor). Any more and the runner dulls before the
  casino escalation; the "vitamin" exchange carries the information.
- The emotional spine (Tommy, playing chicken with the same overdose) remains
  untouched under this ruling, per the note's own constraint.
