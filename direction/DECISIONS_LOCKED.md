# LAST CALL — Locked Creative Decisions
*Master Director Connector v1.0. Standing authority granted: decisions below are
made, not proposed. Optimization target on every call: critical acclaim ×
commercial performance — the four-quadrant deadpan comedy that plays Toronto AND
a Toledo multiplex.*

---

## 1. DALE: NICK OFFERMAN — LOCKED (first offer)

The bridge pick wins because the box-office math and the creative math converge
on the same name:
- **Bankability with credibility:** Offerman closes financing conversations AND
  survives the Act 3 sincerity — *The Last of Us* Ep. 3 proved he can carry grief
  that wins Emmys. He is the only name on either track who de-risks the money
  without risking the tone.
- **Marketing is pre-built:** a flask, a flannel, a deadpan — the persona does
  half the campaign's work. His audience overlaps exactly with the streaming
  true-crime demo the docuseries frame parodies.
- **The sober turn sells:** "Nick Offerman gets terrifyingly competent" is a
  trailer beat, a review pull-quote, and an awards conversation in one.

**Backup ladder:** Shea Whigham → Sam Rockwell → Bill Burr.

## 2. RICKY: JASON MANTZOUKAS — LOCKED (first offer)

- Offerman/Mantzoukas is a poster: order and chaos in one frame. Their comedy-
  podcast-adjacent fanbases are additive, not overlapping — quadrant coverage.
- Nobody alive better plays "announces terrible plans with TED Talk energy," and
  the Perkins/Tommy reveal weaponizes 15 years of audience assumptions about what
  a Mantzoukas character is for. The blindside is the review headline.

**Backup ladder:** Bobby Cannavale → Charlie Day.

## 3. LARRY: J.K. SIMMONS — LOCKED (first offer)

Overruling my earlier John Carroll Lynch lean, on box-office grounds:
- An Oscar winner on the one-sheet, and "I'm not mad, I'm disappointed" from the
  Whiplash actor is a self-marketing trailer moment — the culture does the meme
  work for free.
- The "audience pre-knows he's scary" risk INVERTS into an asset: dread arrives
  with him at the storage unit, so the cocoa offer plays as comedy on top of
  pre-installed menace. We spend less screen time establishing threat.
- The crack beat (Polish ruling #2) in Simmons' hands is the awards clip.

**Backup ladder:** John Carroll Lynch → Tracy Letts → Richard Jenkins.

## 4. CRYSTAL: AUBREY PLAZA — LOCKED (first offer)

Creative and commercial agree with no tension: fourth marketable name, deadpan
authority, and the parking-garage thesis speech becomes a quotable monologue the
press tour runs on. **Backup:** DeWanda Wise → Krysten Ritter.

## 5. DOTTIE: MARGO MARTINDALE — LOCKED. First and only offer, per the board.

## 6. JESSUPS: TIM ROBINSON + CONNER O'MALLEY — LOCKED

The virality engine. "IT DOESN'T EVEN SCAN! THE METER'S ALL WRONG!" is a
clip-economy asset worth more than a TV spot; their internet-native audiences
buy opening-weekend tickets and make the memes that sell week two. Scene-theft
risk is contained by hard scripted beats and the discipline that nobody in this
film knows they're funny. **Backup:** Bennett/Mooney.

## 7. THE THREE POLISH NOTES — RULINGS FINAL

As filed in POLISH_NOTES_DECISIONS.md, now confirmed under the box-office lens:
1. **Crystal's garage scene protected** — it's Plaza's awards/press clip and the
   film's thesis; moving the confession to Ricky spends the bridge scene early.
2. **Larry: one 8-second crack, never a drop** — Simmons casting makes this
   mathematically perfect: maximum dread per second of darkness spent.
3. **Vice calibration holds** — a hard-R Wolf of Wall Street tilt costs us the
   crucial older-female quadrant and the "took my dad, he loved it" word of
   mouth. R-rated for language, never for glamor. The crash-not-the-high
   structure is also what earns the reviews.

## 8. RATING & RUNTIME TARGETS — SET

- **Rating:** R (language, drug content) — but a *soft* R engineered for
  four-quadrant word of mouth. No nudity (Crystal's profession is portrayed
  through Perkins and paperwork — that's the joke AND the rating strategy).
- **Runtime target:** 96 minutes. Comedies die at two hours; 96 buys an extra
  daily showtime per screen and protects the joke density. The editor's north
  star: when in doubt, cut the explanation, keep the deadpan.

## 9. THE ONE NEEDLE-DROP — DECIDED

Dawn drive: **John Denver, "Take Me Home, Country Roads"** as first position —
sincerity that plays both straight and funny, universally known, licensing
achievable at indie scale. Fallback: Bob Seger "Roll Me Away" (Michigan-adjacent
authenticity, trickier license). Score handles everything else.
